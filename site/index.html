01 / THE PHYSICAL IDEA

Move charge. Disturb the field.

A radio doesn’t launch electrons across the room. It moves charge back and forth in conductors, creating changing electric and magnetic fields.

FIRST PRINCIPLES / READ ONE INSTANT

Voltage, charge, and current describe the same motion.

Voltage is a difference in electric potential. Capacitance tells us how much separated charge that voltage produces. Current tells us how fast the charge changes. The derivative below means “rate of change.”

1 / APPLY A VOLTAGE
v(t) = Vpk cos(ωt)

Vpk is peak voltage. ω = 2πf converts cycles per second into radians per second. One cycle is 2π radians, or 360°.

v = +1.00 V
2 / SEPARATE CHARGE
q(t) = C v(t)

C is capacitance. A hypothetical 1 pF capacitance at 1 V holds 1 pC of separated charge. This is an example, not the measured cap capacitance.

q = +1.00 pC
3 / MOVE THAT CHARGE
i(t) = dq/dt
= −ωCVpk sin(ωt)

At the voltage peak, charge briefly stops changing. At the zero crossing, it changes fastest: current reaches its peak. Positive current here charges the cap positively.

i = 0.00 mA

See why current is a quarter-cycle ahead.

1 V peak · 1 pF · 722 MHz · normalized traces
Voltage and chargeCurrent

At 0°, voltage and separated charge peak. Their slope is zero, so capacitor current is zero.

1 pF = 10−12 farads; 1 pC = 10−12 coulombs. The current peak is 2π × 722 MHz × 1 pF × 1 V ≈ 4.54 mA. This is a local charge-storage model, not the full antenna input impedance.

FROM LOCAL MOTION TO A TRAVELING WAVE

The field at a distance responds later.

Moving charge redistributes the electric field, and current creates a magnetic field. The time-varying fields are linked by Maxwell’s equations. A radiating solution carries energy away at a finite speed; it does not require electrons to travel from one antenna to the other.

A FIELD PUSHES CHARGE
F = qE

An arriving electric field E exerts force F on a charge q. Electrons have negative charge, so their force points opposite the field. This is the beginning of reception.

THE WAVE TAKES TIME
E(z,t) = E0 cos[ω(t − z/c)]

The field a distance z away follows an earlier time, t − z/c. In free space, c ≈ 3 × 108 m/s. In one cycle, the wave travels one wavelength: λ = c/f.

ENERGY FLOWS OUTWARD
B = E/c
S = E × H

For a traveling plane wave in free space, E and B are perpendicular and oscillate in phase. The Poynting vector S points along energy flow; H = B/μ0.

A crest travels; an electron stays in its conductor.

Connect this intuition to Maxwell’s equations
∇ × E = −∂B/∂t
∇ × H = J + ∂D/∂t

The curl symbol ∇ × describes how a field circulates around a small loop. A changing magnetic field is associated with circulating electric field. Conduction-current density J and changing electric displacement D are associated with circulating magnetic field. The ∂/∂t notation means change with time.

The term ∂D/∂t is displacement current. It lets current continuity make sense through a capacitor’s insulating gap: charge does not have to conduct across the dielectric. Around an antenna, the full distributed fields and surface currents must be solved together. Ground is the circuit’s reference and return conductor, not a hole into which charge disappears.

Near a small antenna, substantial energy can be stored and returned each cycle. The radiative far-field term falls as 1/r in amplitude and transports power outward. Reactive near-field terms fall faster with distance. Matching changes what the radio sees; it cannot by itself turn all of that stored energy into radiation.

01

There are always two sides.

The radio drives the cap relative to board ground. The ground plane is the counterpoise: the other participating conductor. It is not a connection to the Earth. Fields around the whole structure complete the electromagnetic picture.

02

The space around it counts.

Some field energy stays nearby and returns each cycle. Some propagates away as radiation. The ring, battery, display metal, keys, and your hand change the near field, so they also change the antenna’s impedance and losses.

03

Size is relative to wavelength.

At 722 MHz, the free-space wavelength is about 415 mm. Our 95 mm device is only 0.23 wavelengths long; the 22.5 mm cap alone is 0.054. The design uses the cap to excite currents across a much larger structure.

Go deeper: why a metal part can radiate

An applied voltage redistributes surface charge. Charge creates an electric field; current creates a magnetic field. Changes propagate at finite speed. In a radiating structure, the resulting fields include a component that transports energy away instead of returning all of it to the source.

Along a well-designed transmission line, nearby signal and return conductors keep much of the field guided. At the antenna, the conductor geometry spreads the fields into the surrounding space. Radiation is not an electron leaving the end of a wire, or simply the circuit “running out of ground.”

The diagram emphasizes charge/current timing in one idealized standing-wave mode. Real phase, current distribution, near fields, and radiation patterns require an electromagnetic solution. The end cap is a coupling and loading element in the proposed whole-device antenna.

L302 + C302 / WHY PARALLEL LC IS SPECIAL

Large branch currents can add up to almost no input current.

The coil and capacitor see the same voltage. Their currents point in opposite directions during most of a cycle. At parallel resonance, the ideal currents cancel at the terminals, so the source sees a very high impedance. The pair is connected in series with our RF feed; its two branches are parallel to each other.

IL = 0 mAIC = 0 mAIin = 0 mA

Illustrative 1 V peak across the isolated ideal pair. Arrows show conventional current, with positive defined left to right. Their signs reverse every half-cycle.

+98 ΩInductive at this frequency

iL = sin(ωt)/(ωL)
iC = −ωC sin(ωt)
iin = iL + iC
Zpair = jωL/(1 − ω²LC)

The current equations assume Vpk = 1 V. At f0 = 1/(2π√LC) ≈ 1,299.49 MHz, the current magnitudes match. In real parts, finite Q and parasitic capacitance limit the impedance peak.

Series resonance and parallel resonance do opposite things

Series LC: the same current flows through both parts. Their voltages cancel when ωL = 1/(ωC), so Z = j(ωL − 1/ωC) tends to zero in the ideal case. A series LC branch to ground can make a trap.

Parallel LC: the same voltage appears across both parts. Their currents cancel, so input admittance Y = j(ωC − 1/ωL) tends to zero and impedance tends to infinity. In our proposed series feed element, that peak is between the intended low and high cellular bands.

This does not prove that the assembled antenna matches in either band. Each remaining element and the antenna load still participates. A part’s reactance cannot be interpreted as the entire network’s passband.

Why does an impedance change cause an echo?

V = V+ + V−
I = (V+ − V−)/Z0

A forward wave arrives with a fixed voltage/current ratio set by the line. At the boundary, voltage and current must also satisfy the load. A backward wave supplies the difference. Solving V/I = Zload gives the Γ equation above. The reflected current has the opposite sign because it travels the other way.

Where does “50 Ω” come from?

Z0 ≈ √(L′/C′)
vp ≈ 1/√(L′C′)

L′ and C′ are inductance and capacitance per unit length of a low-loss transmission line. The copper, ground, and dielectric geometry set them. 50 Ω is the interface convention we design around; it is not 50 Ω of copper resistance. Once ground ends near the cap, the exposed feed belongs in the antenna model.

THREE THINGS TO DISCOVER

  1. Canceling reactance is only half the job. A purely resistive 10 Ω load still mismatches a 50 Ω line. The second component transforms the resistance too.
  2. A hand changes two things. Match the bare load, select “Hand nearby,” then retune. You can reduce reflection; the extra absorption remains.
  3. A resistor can fool a match test. Select “50 Ω resistor” and bypass L and C. Zero reflection. Zero radiation. An antenna needs both a good match and useful radiation efficiency.
Show the equations and the model’s assumptions

FIRST DEFINE THE THREE CLOCKS

Carrier frequency sets the reference. Symbols carry the changing information.

Imagine two hands rotating at the same speed. One hand can point a quarter-turn ahead without rotating faster. Within one ideal QPSK symbol, that angular offset is phase. The receiver measures the offset relative to its own synchronized reference.

“Fixed carrier” does not mean the modulated signal contains only one frequency.

Changing symbols changes the waveform, creating energy at frequencies around fc. An unchanging sine conveys no new symbol sequence. θ̂ = atan2(Qp, Ip) − 45°

atan2 uses the signs of both measurements to find the angle. This ideal pilot is noiseless, even when interference is added to the data. Its known phase makes the channel’s added rotation measurable.

Desired signal amplitude = 0.65. Added tones are repeatable interference, not a random-noise or bit-error-rate model. A strong interfering signal can move a point into the wrong quadrant.

Why multiplication reveals phase

r(t) = A cos(ωct + φ)
2r(t)cos(ωct)
= A cos φ + A cos(2ωct + φ)

A trigonometric identity separates a constant term from a fast term at twice the carrier frequency. Averaging removes the fast term in this ideal integer-cycle window, leaving I = A cos φ. The sine branch similarly leaves Q = A sin φ. Then atan2(Q,I) estimates phase.

For the first “H” symbol, 01 means 135°. With amplitude 0.65 and a clean aligned channel, I ≈ −0.460 and Q ≈ +0.460. Their signs put the point in the upper-left quadrant, so the receiver decides 01.

How does the receiver know its reference?

r(t) = A cos(ωct + φ + θ)
Residual phase = θ − θ̂

The path and the receiver oscillator add an unknown phase θ. Known training and reference signals let a receiver estimate it. Here the pilot button supplies a perfect estimate θ̂ = θ; real receivers must estimate phase, frequency offset, timing, and the channel amid noise.

A 90° rotation can make a valid constellation appear to be another valid set of bits. Data points alone do not establish the agreed orientation. The toy receiver makes hard quadrant decisions and has no error correction. Real receivers use soft information, coding, checksums, and retransmissions.

The RF wave carries a coded representation. The application gives the bytes meaning.

ASCII is just the agreed byte-to-character mapping used in this example. An actual boop could be a command field in a packet. LTE adds framing, scrambling, error-control coding, resource mapping, and network procedures. The LC network helps deliver an analog waveform to the radio; it does not decode the message.

WHAT MAKES THIS REAL

Three different measurements answer three different questions.

Does the joint conduct? A continuity test checks the spring, land, and DC return. It cannot establish the RF performance.

Does the radio see a good load? A calibrated VNA measures impedance and reflection at a defined reference plane, with the actual enclosure and nearby objects.

Does the product communicate? Radiated efficiency, transmit power, receive sensitivity, and network tests establish the complete result. A matched resistor passes the second question and fails this one.
